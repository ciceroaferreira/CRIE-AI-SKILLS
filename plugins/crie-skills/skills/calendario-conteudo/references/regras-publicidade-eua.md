# US marketing rules for small-business content

Plain-language reminders, not legal advice. When a post touches health claims, prizes, alcohol, texting lists or
paid partners, tell the user what to check and suggest confirming with an attorney for anything high-stakes.
Federal rules apply in every state; states add their own.

## 1. Truth in advertising (FTC Act §5 + state "little FTC Acts")
- Every objective claim needs proof before posting ("best in Orlando", "#1", "lasts 6 months", "kills 99.9%").
  Prefer opinions and specifics the business can show ("baked fresh every morning at 5am").
- Prices and promos must match what happens at checkout: include fees, end dates, limits ("while supplies last",
  "through 11/30/2026"). A "sale" price needs a real former price. "Free" means free — no hidden purchase.
- Made in USA claims must be "all or virtually all" made in the US (FTC rule).

## 2. Endorsements, influencers and partners (FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255)
- Anyone paid, gifted a product/service, given a discount, or with a family/employment tie must disclose it
  clearly in the post itself: `#ad`, `#sponsored`, "Paid partnership with @brand", "I got this for free from…".
  Use the platform's paid-partnership label too, but it alone may not be enough. Place it at the start of the
  caption and say it in the video — not hidden among hashtags or behind "more". `#sp`, `#collab`, `#thanks` alone
  are not enough.
- The business is responsible for its partners: give them written disclosure instructions and check their posts.
- Employees posting about the business must disclose they work there.

## 3. Reviews and testimonials (FTC Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465, in force since
10/21/2024; civil penalties per violation)
- Never write, buy or ask for fake reviews or testimonials, including AI-written ones or reviews from people who
  did not use the product/service.
- Never offer anything (discount, entry, gift) **in exchange for a positive review**. Google and Yelp go further:
  no incentives for any review, and Yelp does not allow asking for reviews at all. Safest: ask every customer the
  same way, offer nothing.
- No review gating: don't ask only happy customers, or filter unhappy ones away before they can review.
- Don't suppress negative reviews with threats, lawsuits or false reports; don't run a "review site" that is
  secretly the business. Testimonials in posts must be real, typical-or-disclosed results, and with permission.
- Owners, managers and employees must not post reviews of their own business (or of competitors) without
  disclosure — and platforms ban it anyway.

## 4. Sweepstakes, giveaways and contests
- A promotion with a **prize + chance + consideration** (purchase or significant effort) is an illegal lottery.
  So every giveaway that involves chance needs **"No purchase necessary"** and a free way to enter with equal odds.
- Write **official rules**: sponsor name/address, eligibility (age 18+ or 21+, US residents, void where
  prohibited), entry period with time zone, how to enter, free entry method, prize description and approximate
  retail value (ARV), odds, how winners are chosen and notified, publicity release, privacy.
- **New York and Florida**: register (and bond) any game of chance with total prizes over $5,000 — NY at least 30
  days before start, FL at least 7 days before. **Rhode Island**: retail sweepstakes with prizes over $500 must be
  registered. Verify current thresholds with the state before launch; a national promo with large prizes needs a
  lawyer.
- Prizes are taxable income to the winner: above the IRS reporting threshold the sponsor issues a Form 1099-MISC
  ($600 through 2025; $2,000 for payments from 2026 under the 2025 federal tax law — confirm with a CPA) and collects a W-9 first.
- **Skill contests** (best photo, recipe) avoid "chance" but judging criteria must be stated; a few states restrict
  paid-entry skill contests.
- **Instagram/Facebook/TikTok**: the post must release the platform from responsibility and say it isn't sponsored
  or endorsed by it. On Facebook, "share on your timeline to enter" and "tag friends for extra entries" are not
  allowed; on Instagram, don't ask people to tag themselves in photos they aren't in. Check the current Meta and
  TikTok promotion rules before launch.
- Licensed health professionals: don't raffle procedures or prescription treatments (see section 8).

## 5. Text messages (TCPA + carrier rules)
- Marketing texts need **prior express written consent** (checkbox or keyword opt-in that says the customer agrees
  to receive marketing texts, how often, "msg & data rates may apply", "Reply STOP to opt out", not a condition of
  purchase). A customer who texted you a question has not opted in to promotions.
- Honor STOP and other reasonable opt-out requests promptly (FCC rule: within 10 business days at most). Keep
  consent records. Register the number for business texting (10DLC) through the texting platform.
- Send only between 8am and 9pm in the recipient's time zone; some states are stricter (e.g., Florida 8am–8pm and
  max 3 messages per 24h on the same topic; Oklahoma, Washington, Maryland and others have "mini-TCPA" laws).
  Violations cost $500–$1,500 per text.
- Keep SMS short, identify the business, and limit promotions to ~2–4 per month.

## 6. Email (CAN-SPAM)
- Accurate "From" and subject line, identify the message as an ad if it is one, include the business's physical
  postal address and a working unsubscribe link; honor opt-outs within 10 business days. Never buy lists.

## 7. Photos, customers and kids
- Get written permission (a signed release or a recorded "yes" in messages) before posting a customer's face, home,
  car plate or before/after. Ask again if you'll use it in paid ads.
- Minors: written permission from a parent or guardian; never tag a child's location or school. Children's apps
  and sites directed to kids under 13 trigger COPPA.
- Right of publicity: don't use a celebrity's name or image without a license. Use only music the platform
  licenses for business accounts (commercial sound libraries), not trending copyrighted songs, in business posts
  and ads.

## 8. Health, beauty, med spas and supplements
- **FTC**: health claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence. No "cure", "treat", "detox", "melt fat",
  "anti-aging guaranteed", "results in one session" unless proven for that product/procedure. Results must be
  typical or clearly disclosed ("results vary").
- **Before/after photos**: real patients, with written consent (HIPAA authorization if the business is a health
  care provider), same lighting/angle/makeup, no filters or retouching, typical results, and the number of sessions.
  Never use stock or another clinic's photos as your results.
- **Med spas**: procedures like neurotoxins, fillers, lasers, IV therapy and weight-loss injections are the practice
  of medicine or nursing under state law. Ads must not imply a non-licensed person performs them; follow the
  state medical board's advertising rules (Texas: Texas Medical Board rules on false/misleading advertising;
  elective IV therapy outside a physician's office must follow Occupations Code Chapter 172, "Jenifer's Law",
  effective 09/01/2025). Name the licensed provider when claims involve a specialty ("board-certified" only with
  the actual board). Don't raffle or give away prescription treatments; promotional pricing should come with
  "consultation required, not everyone is a candidate".
- **Prescription drugs/brands**: say "Botox®" only for the genuine Allergan product, and don't advertise compounded
  semaglutide/tirzepatide as FDA-approved, "generic Ozempic" or the same as the brand — FDA has warned about this.
  Prescription-only products require an evaluation by a licensed prescriber; say so.
- **Cosmetics (FDA, MoCRA)**: cosmetic claims are about appearance ("looks smoother"); claims to change the body's
  structure or treat conditions ("regrows hair", "removes wrinkles permanently") make it a drug.
- **Supplements (FDA/DSHEA)**: only structure/function claims ("supports energy") with the disclaimer "These
  statements have not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose,
  treat, cure, or prevent any disease." No disease claims.
- Patient testimonials in health care: real, consented, no PHI without HIPAA authorization; don't reply to
  reviews confirming someone is a patient.

## 9. Food and alcohol
- Food: allergen info on request (big 9 allergens, including sesame); cottage-food sellers must follow state label
  rules and sales limits (e.g., Florida cottage food: up to $250,000 gross sales a year, no wholesale, label with "Made in a cottage
  food operation that is not subject to Florida's food safety regulations"; ask FDACS and the destination state
  before shipping out of Florida). "Gluten-free" has an FDA definition.
- Alcohol: federal TTB rules plus each state's ABC/liquor agency. Typical rules: audience 21+ (use age gates and
  platform targeting), no drinking with driving or sports activity, no appeal to minors, no "bottomless"/happy-hour
  promos where the state bans them (e.g., Massachusetts bans happy hours and drink discounts limited to certain
  hours), no giveaways of alcohol in many states. Check the state before any drink promo.

## 10. Other quick checks
- Trademarks: no NFL/Super Bowl, FIFA/World Cup, Olympics, Disney or brand logos; say "the big game".
- Political/election content: stay neutral unless the owner decides otherwise.
- Accessibility: alt text on images, captions on videos, avoid text-only images.
- Licensed professions (lawyers, CPAs, realtors): include license numbers where the state requires them; lawyers
  follow state bar advertising rules ("Attorney Advertising" label in some states; no "specialist" unless certified).

## 11. Insurance, Medicare, financial services, lenders and contractors

These businesses are regulated advertisers: draft the post, mark it ⚠️, and tell the user it must be approved by
their compliance contact (carrier, broker-dealer, compliance officer, managing general agent) before posting.

- **Insurance agents** (state insurance department rules, based on the NAIC unfair trade practices and advertising
  models): no misleading "free" (a free quote is fine; "free coverage" is not), no guarantees beyond what the
  policy says ("guaranteed approval" only for genuinely guaranteed-issue products, with their limits), no
  comparisons that misstate other policies, identify the agent and, when a product is named, the insurer. Many
  carriers require approval of any ad that names them or their products. Show the agent's license number where
  the state requires it (e.g., California requires it on ads and business cards). No "lowest rates" without proof.
  Life insurance: no fear-based or "investment" claims about cash value unless the agent is also securities-licensed
  and the firm approves.
- **Medicare (CMS rules for Medicare Advantage/Part D, 42 CFR 422.2260+ / 423.2260+)**: third-party marketing
  organizations and agents must use the CMS-required disclaimer (e.g., "We do not offer every plan available in your
  area… Contact Medicare.gov or 1-800-MEDICARE to get information on all of your options" — use the exact current
  text); no unsolicited calls, texts, door-to-door or voicemails (people must reach out first or ask to be
  contacted); AEP marketing only from Oct 1; no implying government endorsement or using the Medicare name/logo
  misleadingly; gifts only of nominal value; no meals or gifts tied to enrollment. Medigap/supplement ads also follow
  state rules.
- **Investment advisers (SEC Marketing Rule, 206(4)-1)**: testimonials and endorsements are allowed only with clear
  disclosures (client or not, paid or not, conflicts) and oversight; no performance promises, cherry-picked returns
  or hypothetical performance to the general public; performance shown net of fees with matching periods; keep
  copies of every post, comment reply and ad (books-and-records rules). State-registered advisers follow the state
  securities regulator. **Brokers/registered reps (FINRA Rule 2210)**: retail communications need principal
  approval before posting (some need filing); social posts only through the firm's approved, archived tools.
  "Likes" or sharing client testimonials can count as adopting them.
- **Lenders, mortgage brokers, auto/consumer credit (TILA/Regulation Z)**: rates must be stated as APR. If an ad
  uses a **trigger term** — down payment amount or %, number of payments or term, payment amount, or finance charge
  amount — it must also disclose the down payment, terms of repayment and APR (and whether it may increase). Fair
  Housing Act and ECOA: no language that prefers or excludes protected groups, targeting settings that exclude
  them, or "perfect for young couples"; mortgage lenders display "Equal Housing Lender" (or Opportunity) and NMLS ID
  where required. No "guaranteed approval" or "no credit check" unless literally true and legal in that state.
- **Credit repair (CROA + state laws)**: no promises to remove accurate negative items, no "new credit identity",
  and no fees charged before the service is fully performed.
- **Tax preparers**: no "guaranteed biggest refund", no implying IRS endorsement; IRS Circular 230 and FTC rules
  apply.
- **Contractors/builders**: show the license or registration number in ads where the state requires it (e.g.,
  California, Florida; Massachusetts Home Improvement Contractor registration); "licensed and insured" only if true;
  before/after photos must be your own real projects (no stock or other contractors' work) with the homeowner's
  permission; no "lifetime warranty" unless the written warranty says so; financing ads follow Reg Z above.
