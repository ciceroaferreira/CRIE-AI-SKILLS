## How the business works
- Revenue: fixed-price jobs from estimates, time and materials, change orders, service calls (trades), subcontract work for GCs (often with retainage of 5–10% held until completion).
- Costs: labor and subcontractors, materials (volatile prices), equipment, vehicles, insurance, permits.
- Cash flow is the main risk: deposits, progress payments, slow-paying GCs and retainage. Seasonality in cold states.
- Winners: accurate estimates with margin, written contracts and change orders, progress billing, safety culture, and a pipeline of referrals and reviews.

## Key metrics
- Gross margin per job (estimated vs. actual)
- Estimate win rate and backlog (signed work ahead, in weeks)
- Change orders billed vs. extra work done for free
- Accounts receivable aging and retainage outstanding
- Labor hours per job vs. estimate; callbacks/warranty visits
- Safety incidents and OSHA recordables

## Regulation, confidentiality and risks
- Licensing varies by state and town; verify yours. Examples: Massachusetts HIC registration (home improvement) plus Construction Supervisor License (CSL) for structural work; New Jersey HIC registration; Florida CILB state certification/registration (plus local licenses); California CSLB license (required for jobs of $1,000+ in labor and materials since 2025, or any job that needs a permit). Electrical, plumbing and HVAC need separate trade licenses.
- Permits and inspections: pull the permit before starting, never cover work before inspection, keep the permit card and final sign-off in the job file.
- Written home-improvement contracts: many states require specific clauses (registration number, start/completion dates, total price and payment schedule, scope, right to cancel, guaranty fund notice in MA). Some states cap the upfront deposit (e.g., Massachusetts: the greater of one-third of the price or the cost of special-order materials). Florida: contracts over $2,500 need the Homeowners' Construction Recovery Fund notice and the s. 713.015 lien-law disclosure; more than 10% up front means permits applied for within 30 days and work started within 90 days of the permit (s. 489.126).
- Change orders: in writing, signed before the extra work, with price and schedule impact.
- Mechanics' liens: preliminary notice / notice to owner deadlines (e.g., Florida Notice to Owner, California preliminary notice) and lien filing deadlines vary by state; collect conditional and unconditional lien waivers with each payment. Confirm deadlines with a construction attorney.
- Retainage: often 5–10% held until completion; some states cap it or set release deadlines (verify).
- Insurance: general liability, workers' comp (required in most states with employees), commercial auto, builder's risk for new construction/major renovation; collect certificates of insurance (COIs) from every sub.
- Subs: W-9 before the first payment and 1099-NEC at year-end; misclassifying workers as 1099 is a major risk (strict ABC tests in MA, NJ, CA). Confirm with your CPA/attorney.
- OSHA: fall protection (leading cause of construction deaths), ladders, scaffolds, silica, PPE; OSHA 10/30 training records.
- EPA RRP (Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting) rule: work that disturbs paint in pre-1978 homes or child-occupied facilities needs an EPA-certified firm and renovator, the Renovate Right pamphlet, lead-safe practices and records.
- Warranty/workmanship: state the workmanship warranty in writing (term, exclusions, how to file a claim); some states imply warranties on new homes.
- Data: client home addresses, gate/lockbox codes, employee data, bank info for progress payments (watch for wire fraud: confirm changes by phone) → `elevated`.
